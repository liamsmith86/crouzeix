# Uniform division by the disk equality quartic (L155, 2026-07-23)

## 1. The analytic lemma

Let

\[
{\cal C}z=J\overline z,\qquad
{\cal E}=\{z:z=\omega{\cal C}z,\ |\omega|=1\},
\]

and

\[
{\cal Q}(z)=\|z\|^4-|z^TJz|^2.
\]

Let `F` be a real-valued real-analytic germ at the origin of
`C^(L-1)`.  Suppose:

1. `F(u)=0` for every `u in E`; and
2. `DF(u)=0` for every `u in E`.

Then there are `epsilon_L,C_L>0` such that

\[
\boxed{
|F(z)|\le C_L{\cal Q}(z)
\qquad(\|z\|<\epsilon_L).
}                                                     \tag{1}
\]

The result is equally valid for a complex-valued analytic germ after
applying it to the real and imaginary parts.

This is a uniform analytic division estimate.  It does not assert
literal divisibility by the single scalar polynomial `Q`; the
codimension of the equality cone is generally larger than one.
Algebraically, the fourth-order face may be any quadratic form in the
`2 x 2` minors of the two-row matrix
`[z; J conjugate(z)]`.

## 2. Best-phase normal coordinates

L124 gives, for every `z`, an antiunitary involution `T` and a
real-orthogonal splitting

\[
z=u+v,\qquad Tu=u,\quad Tv=-v,\quad\|v\|\le\|u\|,
                                                               \tag{2}
\]

such that `u in E` and

\[
\boxed{
{\cal Q}(z)=4\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
}                                                     \tag{3}
\]

The phase in (2) need not vary analytically with `z`.  The proof below
uses the splitting only pointwise, so this causes no coordinate
problem.

## 3. First-order cone vanishing forces a fourth-order apex

The former separate hypothesis `F(z)=O(||z||^4)` is automatic.
After a real-orthogonal change of coordinates, the antiunitary
involution `C` is ordinary coordinatewise conjugation.  Writing
`z=x+iy`, the equality cone becomes

\[
 {\cal E}=\{(x,y):x\wedge y=0\};                     \tag{4}
\]

it is the real rank-one cone of the generic `2 x (L-1)` matrix whose
rows are `x` and `y`.

Write `F=sum_(d>=0) F_d` as its convergent expansion into real
homogeneous polynomials.  Scaling any point of `E` and using both
hypotheses gives, degree by degree,

\[
 F_d|_{\cal E}=0,\qquad DF_d|_{\cal E}=0.             \tag{5}
\]

The real rank-one points are Zariski dense in the complex rank-one
determinantal variety.  Its prime ideal `I` is generated by the
quadratic minors `x_i y_j-x_j y_i`.  Equation (5) puts `F_d` in the
second symbolic power `I^(2)`.  For the ideal of maximal minors of a
generic `2 x m` matrix, the standard straightening law gives

\[
 I^{(2)}=I^2.                                        \tag{6}
\]

Since `I` is generated in degree two, `I^2` has no nonzero homogeneous
element below degree four.  Therefore

\[
\boxed{F_0=F_1=F_2=F_3=0,\qquad F(z)=O(\|z\|^4).}    \tag{7}
\]

Only this low-degree consequence of (6) is needed.  It also explains
why the independent exact checker always finds the subquartic apex
jet zero once the full equality-cone gradient vanishes.

## 4. Fourth-order derivative bounds

Equation (7) and Taylor's theorem give, after shrinking a fixed ball,

\[
\boxed{
\|D^jF(x)\|\le C\|x\|^{4-j},
\qquad j=0,1,2,3,
}                                                     \tag{8}
\]

for all `x` in that ball.

For completeness, write

\[
F(x)=\sum_{d\ge4}F_d(x)
\]

as its convergent homogeneous expansion.  On a smaller ball, the
series obtained by differentiating `j<=3` times converges normally.
Each term is bounded by
`C_d||x||^(d-j)`.  Factoring `||x||^(4-j)` and summing the remaining
geometric majorant proves (8).

## 5. Taylor expansion normal to the cone

Use (2).  The two hypotheses give

\[
F(u)=0,\qquad DF(u)[v]=0.
\]

Taylor only in the real normal direction:

\[
F(u+v)
=\frac12D^2F(u)[v,v]
+\frac16D^3F(u+\theta v)[v,v,v]                    \tag{9}
\]

for some `0<theta<1`.  Equations (2) and (8) imply

\[
\begin{aligned}
|D^2F(u)[v,v]|
&\le C\|u\|^2\|v\|^2,\\
|D^3F(u+\theta v)[v,v,v]|
&\le C(\|u\|+\|v\|)\|v\|^3\\
&\le2C\|u\|\|v\|^3
\le2C\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
\end{aligned}                                       \tag{10}
\]

Combining (3), (9), and (10) proves (1).  No separate angular
compactness or Łojasiewicz exponent is needed.

## 6. Application to reflected coefficients

Let `F_k(z)` be the coefficient of one independent reflected Hardy
variable of grade `k` in any real-analytic Crabb certificate.  If

\[
\boxed{
F_k|_{\cal E}=0,\qquad
DF_k|_{\cal E}=0,
}                                                     \tag{11}
\]

then L155 gives

\[
|F_k(z)|\le C_{L,k}{\cal Q}(z).                     \tag{12}
\]

There are only finitely many grades in a fixed size.  On the physical
elliptic family, the corresponding variable contains `c^k`.
Consequently

\[
\left|\sum_k c^kF_k(z)\right|
\le C_L|c|{\cal Q}(z)                               \tag{13}
\]

for `|c|<1`.  Shrinking `|c|` absorbs (13) into any fixed fraction of
L152's negative disk-normal term `-a_L Q`.

Thus the corrected disk-flat merger does **not** need exact
one-reflection stationarity at a general disk point, nor a separate
apex recurrence.  It needs only the two equality-cone jets in (11).
L148/L149 already give the value condition.
`experiments/crabb_disk_one_reflection_jets.py` evaluates the full
root-free recurrence over an exact truncated-series ring.  It
verifies the gradient condition at deterministic equality/normal data
and independently confirms the now-redundant subquartic apex
cancellation through length ten.  Those finite records are
adversarial evidence; the equality-gradient assertion remains to be
proved all-size.

## 7. Scope

L155 is purely analytic and dimension-by-dimension.  Its constant may
depend on `L`, which is sufficient for the local fixed-size Crabb
classification.  It does not by itself address a dimension-uniform
global Crouzeix constant.

The lemma also applies to primal/dual model gaps or optimized-envelope
coefficients.  Once (11) is established for whichever explicit
certificate is used, no further singular-cone remainder argument is
required.
